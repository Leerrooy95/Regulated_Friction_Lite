# The Structure

**What this is**: the plain-language account of what the research found once you stop watching the friction and start watching what moves while everyone's looking at it. It replaces the "thermostat" language the project used to lean on. That metaphor was doing too much work: it implied a single mechanism with something running it, which is more than the evidence supports.

What's below isn't a metaphor. It's a list of documented changes, and of what's been removed that would normally slow any one of them down. It's the same idea as "The Wave" in [`Baseline_and_Learnings.md`](Baseline_and_Learnings.md), just laid out piece by piece with the sourcing attached.

---

## The short version

Several groups are moving the same direction at once. Each of the changes below was a deliberate choice by someone. None of this happened by accident. What this doesn't claim is that the separate groups are coordinating with each other. They don't need to be. Each one has its own reason to want what it wants.

What changed isn't that they started agreeing. What changed is that the things that used to slow them down have been removed one at a time, mostly in public, by different hands, in the same stretch of time.

That's the finding.

---

## 1. Removing the people who can say no

The most concrete piece, and the best documented.

| What | When | What it did |
|---|---|---|
| **DOGE** | 2025 (absorbed into OPM Nov 2025) | Presented as efficiency work. In practice it cleared institutional obstacles ahead of restructuring. The repo tracks the DOGE → OPM → DOJ → FBI sequence as a closed loop, where each step made the next one easier. |
| **Schedule Policy/Career** (the revived Schedule F) | Final rule Feb 5, 2026; effective Mar 9, 2026 | Converted roughly **50,000 career federal positions** to at-will status and removed their right to appeal to the MSPB. Public comments on the rule ran **94% against**. |
| **MSPB caseload** | FY2025 | Appeals to the Merit Systems Protection Board hit **20,335** for the year — roughly **four times** the normal annual volume of ~5,677 (FY2024), a ~258% increase — from the earlier mass firings. That's the same board the new rule then took those 50,000 positions out of reach of. |

Read those three together and the shape is simple. The civil servant who might refuse an order, and the board they'd appeal to if fired for refusing, were both dealt with in the same window.

The repo's own name for this is **defense mechanism elimination**: earlier patterns in this project worked *around* checks by timing actions for when nobody was looking. This one removes the checks outright. See "Administrative State Audit" in [`AI_Context_Index.md`](AI_Context_Index.md).

**The honest counterpoint**: every administration reorganizes the executive branch, and every one calls it efficiency. What sets this apart is the appeal-rights piece. Reorganizations usually move people around. They don't usually remove the way to contest the move. That's the part worth arguing about, and the part to defend if someone pushes back.

## 2. Putting private people inside public authority

The opposite direction from §1: instead of removing government people, bringing non-government people into government roles.

**PCAST**, the President's Council of Advisors on Science and Technology, was re-established by executive order on January 23, 2025. The first 13 of up to 24 members were named March 25, 2026, including **Larry Ellison, Mark Zuckerberg, and Jensen Huang**. That's a formal advisory seat on AI and crypto policy, held by the people who sell the AI and crypto products that policy governs.

Advisory councils are normal. What's worth noticing is who's on this one and what it advises on.

The same move shows up one layer down in the money: the OPM Director who signed the Schedule Policy/Career rule, Scott Kupor, spent 2009–2025 as a founding employee and managing partner at Andreessen Horowitz. The repo documents that relationship without claiming it proves anything about intent (same entry in [`AI_Context_Index.md`](AI_Context_Index.md)).

## 3. What happens to someone in the majority who breaks ranks

The clearest single case in the whole project, because it works like a comparison. Four Republicans signed the discharge petition that forced a vote on the Epstein Files Transparency Act, alongside all 214 Democrats. The bill passed the House 427–1 and was signed into law November 19, 2025.

Here's where those four were by mid-2026:

| Who | What happened |
|---|---|
| **Thomas Massie** | Lost his primary May 19, 2026, final certified 54.9%–45.1%, to a Trump-endorsed challenger. The race cost **$32–37M**, the most expensive House primary in U.S. history, with **$15.5M** of the opposition spending coming from three pro-Israel-aligned super PACs and allied megadonors. |
| **Marjorie Taylor Greene** | Resigned from Congress in 2026 after repeated breaks with Trump, Epstein among them. |
| **Nancy Mace** | Left the House to run for South Carolina governor, finished fifth in that primary, and said directly that losing Trump's endorsement over the Epstein vote cost her the race. |
| **Lauren Boebert** | Survived. She wasn't spared. She was targeted too, especially after campaigning for Massie, but the operation moved too late for a challenger to file. She ran unopposed. |

Three of four gone. The fourth survived because of filing deadlines, not because she was left alone.

**The honest counterpoint**: primaries are lost for lots of reasons, and each of these four had other political problems. It still holds up as evidence because of the fourth case. Boebert's survival comes down to timing, not to anything different about her record. That's what you'd expect if the common factor was the targeting, not the individuals.

## 4. Why the other side isn't working as a brake

⚠️ **This section is interpretation, not a documented finding. It's marked that way on purpose.**

What's documented: the discharge petition needed 218 signatures. All 214 Democrats signed and it still wasn't enough. It took four Republicans crossing over to force the vote. In a chamber where the majority controls the floor schedule, a unified minority can't schedule anything on its own.

What's inference: that one side moves as a bloc while the other's loyalties are split enough that it can't push back the same way. That reading fits the Epstein vote, but one vote isn't a pattern. The ordinary explanation also fits the same facts: minority parties have few tools no matter how unified they are.

If this is going to carry weight in the argument, it needs its own evidence: party-unity scores for both parties over several sessions, and a count of how often each side's defectors faced organized primary challenges. That's a real research task and it hasn't been done yet.

## 5. The same shape, one level down

Arkansas is the state-level version. It's the best-documented case in the project because it's local and the records are public.

| Piece | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Act 373 (2025)** | A utility whose application is denied can resubmit indefinitely. The law provides **no path to a final denial**, only approval, withdrawal, or appeal. The Public Service Commission approved the $1.5B Jefferson Power Station in January 2026 while its own order called the cost "not reasonable," after the Attorney General's expert and PSC staff both recommended denial. |
| **Act 548 (2025)** | Dropped the data-center tax-exemption threshold from $500M to $100M, and created a tier that lets one company combine separate, non-adjacent sites into a single exempt project. |
| **Dec 11, 2025 state Supreme Court ruling** | Ended 74 years of precedent protecting voter-approved constitutional amendments. The legislature can now amend or repeal a citizen initiative with a two-thirds vote. |
| **Ballot-access restrictions + ongoing appeal** | Several 2025 laws narrowed the initiative process. Six were blocked as likely unconstitutional in November 2025, but the state's appeal keeps the rules uncertain past the July 3, 2026 signature deadline. That runs out the clock no matter how the case ends. |

What arrived alongside it: **$17-19B** in announced initial data-center investment (AVAIO $6B initial/$21B at full buildout, Google West Memphis $4B, Serverfarm Clarksville $6.6B, plus $1B each for Google's Little Rock Port project and an unnamed Conway project) — the total is a moving target: Serverfarm's own figure has been reported as high as $8B and as low as $6.6B across 2025-2026 coverage, and Arkansas Advocate reporting from August 2026 puts the statewide count at six proposed projects, not five, so this number likely undercounts as of September 2026. The incentive agreements put the permanent job counts in writing, and they're small. Two of the projects are $1B each with **50 permanent jobs each** and a 65% property-tax abatement for 30 years, which works out to about $20M of investment per job. The largest, AVAIO's $21B Little Rock buildout, promises 500+. There's also a defense-manufacturing buildout in the same part of the state: L3Harris in Camden, the Raytheon–Rafael missile joint venture, and Hanwha at Pine Bluff Arsenal.

Jobs are the stated reason for all of it. The job numbers are right there in the public paperwork.

**Why this is the strongest section**: it doesn't need any claim about intent. A commission approving a project it called unreasonable isn't evidence of corruption. It's the predictable result of a law written so that denial only delays. The way the law works is the finding.

## 6. What the timing research adds

The correlation work still stands: friction events come before compliance events with a 7-day median lag, r = +0.6196, p = 0.0004, and it held up under sixteen independent robustness tests. But it answers a narrower question than this document does.

The statistics measure *when* things happen. This document explains *why the timing would matter*: once the checks that would slow an action are gone, the main thing left that can slow it down is public attention. Things move when nobody's looking because attention is the last brake still working.

That's a clearer statement of the original idea than the thermostat ever was, and it doesn't need anyone to be running anything.

The statistics are in [`Core_Theory.md`](Core_Theory.md). The honest version of how much the 7-day number can carry is under "Verification and Methodology" in [`AI_Context_Index.md`](AI_Context_Index.md).

## 7. What this does NOT claim

- ❌ **No claim that the groups are coordinating with each other.** Each change above was deliberate, but nothing here needs the separate groups to be in contact. Each has its own reason. That's the point: coordination isn't needed to explain the direction.
- ❌ **No claim that any of it is illegal.** Most of what's described is lawful on its face. Acts 373 and 548 were passed by a legislature. The PCAST appointments are within the president's authority. That's what makes it structural rather than criminal.
- ❌ **No claim about any one person's motives.** Why a given legislator sponsored a given bill isn't documented anywhere and isn't claimed here.
- ❌ **No claim that every piece is new.** Reorganizations, advisory councils, and primary challenges are all normal on their own. The argument is about what happens when they run together and the appeal mechanisms disappear at the same time.

## 8. Not in here yet (on purpose)

Two pieces fit this picture but are left out until they have proper sourcing in the main repo:

- **Project 2025 (Heritage Foundation).** The plan to compare against. Right now the main repo mentions it only once, in a file the author already flagged as unverified AI output. The useful version would map specific proposals to specific actions, including the ones that *weren't* carried out, since the hit rate is the whole story.
- **Detachment 201.** The Army program commissioning tech executives directly as Reserve lieutenant colonels. It's covered in `Baseline_and_Learnings.md`, but there's no sourced main-repo file behind it yet.

Neither one changes the argument above. Both would show that this goes beyond a handful of cases: a policy-level pattern of privatization and planning. They get added once they're sourced.

The party-cohesion point in §4 also needs its own evidence before it's more than a reading.

## 9. What would change the read

The structural argument fails if:

- The appeal-rights and MSPB changes get reversed or seriously weakened in court, and civil-service protections work again.
- The Arkansas initiative case ends with the restrictions struck down *and* a ballot measure actually reaches voters. That would mean the democratic check survived.
- Members who break with leadership stop losing primaries at an unusual rate, especially if a future defector faces no organized challenge.

Any one of those would mean revising this document, not defending it.
